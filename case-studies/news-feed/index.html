Case study 03 \u00b7 Advanced

Design a social news feed

The fan-out trade-off: precompute every feed, or assemble it at read time?

45 min 5 requirements 4 layers 3 trade-offs
Design prompt27 min · say it out loud

Design a social feed where users follow other users. The feed shows recent posts ranked by relevance. Some accounts have 50 million followers. Users refresh often and expect new posts to appear quickly.

Take it to the board

1. Requirements

Start with what the system must do, then separate it from how well it must do it. These are the functional promises this design keeps.

  • Publish text and image posts.
  • Follow and unfollow accounts.
  • Show a ranked home feed with cursor pagination.
  • Support celebrity accounts with tens of millions of followers.
  • Keep the feed usable when the ranking service is slow.

2. Constraints and assumptions

Every estimate below depends on these. If the interviewer changes one, the design should change with it.

  • Feed reads outnumber posts by more than 100:1.
  • A feed request should return in under 300 ms at p95.
  • Ranking can tolerate a short staleness window; a new post should appear within seconds.
  • Users should not see the same post twice in one session.

3. Back-of-the-envelope estimates

Daily readers100M

Assume 30% active at peak, 10 refreshes each.

Posts~2,300 / s

200M posts per day, 2x peak.

Feed reads~350k / s

The read path dominates by orders of magnitude.

4. The shape

Read the diagram from the caller to the data. The interesting part is what crosses a boundary and what happens when it does not arrive.

5. API surface

A small, explicit contract. Each endpoint should say what it accepts, what it returns and whether repeating it is safe.

MethodPathPurpose
POST/postsCreate a post and emit a fan-out event.
GET/feed?cursor=Return a ranked page of feed items.
POST/follows/:userIdFollow an account and update the graph.
DELETE/follows/:userIdUnfollow and stop future fan-out.

6. Data model

The data model is where most of the design hides. Name the key, the access pattern and what grows without bound.

Table / streamFields and notes
postspost_id, author_id, body, media_ids, created_at
feed_entriesuser_id (partition), post_id (sort), score, inserted_at
social_graphfollower_id, followee_id, created_at (both directions indexed)

7. Fan-out on write is not enough

The naive design writes a post id into every follower's feed at publish time. That is excellent for reads and terrible for a celebrity: one post becomes 50 million writes. The opposite design reads every followee's recent posts at request time, which is excellent for publishing and expensive for users who follow thousands of accounts.

The practical answer is hybrid. Precompute feed entries for normal accounts. For accounts above a follower threshold, skip fan-out and merge their recent posts into the reader's feed at request time. The read path becomes a k-way merge plus ranking.

8. Ranking is a separate service

Store a lightweight candidate set first, then rank. The feed API should be able to serve chronological or fallback results when the ranking service is unavailable. This is graceful degradation, not a second product.

Keep ranking features near the candidate generation layer. Fetching dozens of features per post at request time will blow the latency budget. Precompute what you can and cap the candidate set.

  • Candidate generation: precomputed entries plus celebrity merge.
  • Filtering: blocks, mutes, deleted posts, duplicate ids.
  • Ranking: bounded feature lookup with a timeout and fallback.

9. Pagination and freshness

Offset pagination is unstable in a live feed. Use a cursor that encodes the last score and id. When new posts arrive, the next page can still be fetched without duplicates. A session-level seen set can remove duplicates introduced by ranking refreshes.

A user who refreshes expects new content. Separate the refresh from the pagination cursor: new items go on top, while the existing page cursor continues backwards. This is a product decision that makes the data model simpler.

Decisions and their costs

There is no free architecture. Each row is a choice, the situation that makes it reasonable, and the bill that arrives later.

01

Hybrid fan-out

Choose it when

Follower counts are highly skewed and read latency matters.

What it costs

Two feed assembly paths and a merge step at read time.

02

Precomputed feed ids

Choose it when

Most users follow a manageable number of accounts.

What it costs

Fan-out work and storage proportional to follower count.

03

Fallback ranking

Choose it when

Ranking is an enhancement, not a correctness requirement.

What it costs

You need a well-defined chronological or affinity fallback.

What breaks first

Failure is normal. The useful question is not whether a component fails, but what the user sees when it does.

FailureImpactMitigation
Fan-out workers fall behindNew posts appear late in precomputed feeds.Monitor lag, merge recent posts from followees at read time as a backstop.
Ranking service times outFeed latency spikes.Short timeout, circuit breaker, chronological fallback.
Celebrity post causes a read spikeHot post metadata is fetched repeatedly.Cache the recent celebrity post set and replicate hot keys.
